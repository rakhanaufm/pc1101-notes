# 4D-Notation
## 4-Velocity
The four-velocity is the four-vector whose components $u^\mu$ are the derivatives of the position along the world line with respect to the proper time parameter $\tau$:
$$
u^\mu \equiv \frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}
$$
The four-velocity is always a constant-length time-like four-vector:
$$
\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathbf{u} = \frac{ds^{2}}{d\tau^{2}} = -c^{2}
$$
the four velocity is tangent to the world line at each point:
$$
\Delta x^\mu = u_\mu\Delta \tau
$$
In terms of the three-velocity in a particular inertial frame: $V^i = \frac{dx^i}{dt}$
$$
d\tau = \sqrt{ 1 - \frac{V^{2}}{c^{2}} }dt
$$
$$
\Rightarrow
\begin{cases} 
u^0 = \frac{dx^0}{d\tau} = \frac{cdt}{d\tau} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{ 1 - \frac{V^{2}}{c^{2}} }} = \gamma c  \\
u^i = \frac{dx^i}{d\tau} = \frac{dt}{d\tau} \frac{dx^i}{dt} = \gamma V^i
\end{cases} 
$$

## 4-Acceleration
The four-acceleration is the four vector whose components $a^\mu$ are the derivatives of the four-velocity along the world line with respect to the proper time parameter $\tau$:
$$
a^\mu \equiv \frac{du^\mu}{d\tau}
$$
It follows that
$$
\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{u} = 0
$$
## 4-Momentum
The four-momentum or energy-momentum four-vector is defined by:
$$
p^\mu \equiv mu^\mu
$$
The constant $m$, which characterizes the particle's inertial properties, is called the rest mass.
It follows that
$$
\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{p} = m^{2} \mathbf{u}\cdot \mathbf{u} = -m^{2}c^{2}
$$
In terms of the three-velocity $V^i = \frac{dx^i}{dt}$ in a particular inertial frame:
$$
p^0 = \mu^0 = \gamma mc \equiv \frac{E}{c}
$$
$$
p^i = \mu^i = \gamma mV^i
$$
# Relativistic Momentum
Classical: $\vec{p} = m \vec{v}$
Relativistic correct:
$$
\vec{p} = \gamma m \vec{p}
$$
## Kinetic Energy (1)
Non-relativistic: $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^{2}$
Relativistic: need to consider the contribution of the mass to the energy
Energy contained in the mass of a particle
$$
E_{0} = mc^{2}
$$
This is the energy that a particle has when it is at rest; sometimes also called "rest energy"
When a particle is in motion, then its energy increases, just like the time becomes dilated:
$$
E = \gamma E_{0} = \gamma mc^{2}
$$
## Kinetic Energy (2)
From the energy, we can obtain the kinetic energy by subtraction of the rest energy:
$$
K = E - E_{0} = (\gamma-1)E_{0} = (\gamma-1)mc^{2}
$$
Non-relativistic limit $(v \ll c)$:
$$
\beta = \frac{v}{c}\ll 1
$$
$$
\gamma = (1-\beta^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \approx 1 + \frac{1}{2} \beta^{2} + \dots = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}
$$
$$
K = (\gamma-1) mc^{2} \approx \frac{1}{2} \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}} mc^{2} = \frac{1}{2}mv^{2}
$$
## Momentum and Energy (1)
Start with the results for momentum and energy and square:
$$
\vec{p} = \gamma m \vec{v} \Rightarrow p^{2} = \gamma^{2}m^{2}v^{2}
$$
$$
E = \gamma mc^{2} \Rightarrow E^{2} = \gamma^{2} m^{2}c^4
$$
Square of the gamma-factor:
$$
\gamma^{2} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta^{2}} = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}} = \frac{c^{2}}{c^{2} - v^{2}}
$$
Express $E^{2}$ as a function of $p^{2}$:
$$
E^{2} = \frac{c^{2}}{c^{2} - v^{2}} m^{2}c^4 = m^{2}c^4 + \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}-v^{2}}m^{2}c^4
$$
$$
m^{2}c^4 + \frac{c^{2}}{c^{2}-v^{2}}m^{2}v^{2}c^{2} = m^{2}c^4 + \gamma^{2}m^{2}v^{2}c^{2}
$$
$$
E^{2} = m^{2}c^4 + p^{2}c^{2}
$$
## Momentum and Energy (2)
Finally we take the square root
$$
E = \sqrt{ p^{2}c^{2} + m^{2}c^4 }
$$
Special case with no motion:
$$
E = mc^{2}
$$
Negative root: antimatter (Dirac)
$$
E = - \sqrt{ p^{2}c^{2} + m^{2}c^4 }
$$
Case of zero mass (photons):
$$
E = pc
$$
## Speed, Energy, and Momentum
Divide momentum by energy
$$
\frac{p}{E} = \frac{\gamma m v}{\gamma mc^{2}} = \frac{v}{c^{2}} \Rightarrow \frac{pc^{2}}{E}
$$
This gives us an additional energy-momentum relationship:
$$
\beta = \frac{pc}{E} \Rightarrow p = \frac{\beta E}{c} or E = \frac{pc}{\beta}
$$
#  Newton's 1st Law of Motion
In the absence of forces, a body is at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed:
$$
\frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} = 0
$$
This implies $V^i$ is a constant in any inertial frame
$$
\begin{cases}
u^0 = \gamma c = \frac{c}{\sqrt{ 1 - \frac{V^{2}}{c^{2}} }} \\
u^i = \gamma V^i 
\end{cases}
$$
In terms of four-momentum,
$$
\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau} = 0
$$
## Newton's 2nd Law of Motion
The four-force $f^\mu$ is defined as:
$$
f^\mu \equiv \frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}
$$
It follows that
$$
f \cdot p = 0
$$
In a particular inertial frame, we have the components
$$
f^0 = \frac{\gamma}{c}\sum_{i} F^iV^i, \space f^i = \gamma F^i
$$
where
$$
F^i \equiv \frac{dp^i}{dt}
$$
## Tangent 4-Vector of a Photon
Photons (light rays) move at the speed of light $c$ along null world lines.
The proper time interval $\tau_{AB}$ between any 2 points on the world line of a light ray is zero - it can no longer be used as a parameter along it!
Tangent four-vector:
$$
x^\mu = \lambda u^\mu, \space u^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}
$$
Note: Different choices of parametrization $\lambda$ will give different $u^\mu$

It follows that for all $\mathbf{u}$:
$$
u \cdot u = 0
$$
and, the equation of motion of a light ray for this choice of parameter:
$$
\frac{du^\mu}{d\lambda} = 0
$$
Affine parameter:
$$
\sigma = k_{0} + k_{1}\lambda
$$
$$
\Rightarrow \frac{du^\mu}{d\sigma} = 0
$$
## 4-Momentum of a Photon
Photons carry energy
$$
E = \hbar \omega = \hbar ck^0 = p^0 c
$$
and three-momentum
$$
p^i = \hbar k^i
$$
Note: $k^i$ points in the direction of propagation.
In any inertial frame:
$$
p \cdot p = - \frac{\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}{c^{2}} + \hbar^{2}\vec{k}\cdot \vec{k} = 0
$$
$$
\therefore | \vec{k}| = \frac{\omega}{c}
$$
## Newton's 1sr Law for Photon
Both $p^\mu$ and $k^\mu$ are tangent to the world line of a photon
The tangent vector $u^\mu$ could be chosen to coincide with either $p^\mu$ or $u^\mu$ by adjusting the normalization of the affine parameter $\lambda$.
Newton's 1st law:
$$
\frac{dp^\mu}{d\lambda} = 0, \frac{dk^\mu}{d\lambda} = 0
$$
